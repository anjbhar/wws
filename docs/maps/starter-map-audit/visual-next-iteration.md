# Starter courtyard — visual correction brief

October 22, 2026. Scope: terrain, architecture, and props in the current courtyard. Based on the fresh engine [initial view](initial.png) and [close-up views](contact-sheet.png). These are visual judgments for the next art pass, not gameplay or event findings.

The main visual problem is that the courtyard reads as a clean rectangular grass field with a dirt patch and separately placed objects. The next pass should make the ground, enclosure, hall, and props feel like one constructed and regularly used place. Keep the sparse layout and the original hall dimensions; more decoration is not the solution.

## Terrain

### 1. The practice ground is too geometrically regular — first priority

The long straight dirt edges, similar rounded corners, and mirrored outline make it look like an inset mat or a prepared garden bed. It does not suggest repeated footwork.

**Next fix:** break the straight edges with shallow, uneven turf intrusions and small worn extensions. Concentrate wear where people train and enter. Keep the broad footprint, but avoid four equally treated corners and a uniform border.

**Acceptance:** at native game scale, the shape reads as worn ground without needing high-contrast stones, dark outlines, or scattered noise.

### 2. Grass and dirt meet too abruptly

The boundary changes from green to tan over a narrow, fairly consistent edge. The two surfaces look like adjacent filled shapes instead of intermingling soil and turf.

**Next fix:** vary the transition width. Use a few thin grass patches within the soil and sparse exposed earth immediately outside it. Keep these details grouped rather than outlining the whole patch with grass tufts.

### 3. The grass texture is too uniform across the yard

The same fine, evenly distributed light flecks appear across large uninterrupted areas. This creates a wallpaper effect and makes sheltered ground beside walls look much like open ground.

**Next fix:** quiet the fleck contrast and introduce broad, subtle variation. Use slightly different wear near the deck, gate, and training objects. Avoid random individual marks across every tile.

### 4. The ground does not connect the important structures

The stones enter the lower end of the dirt patch, while the space between its northern edge and the dojo remains an even grass strip. The props also sit on largely identical grass regardless of how they would be used.

**Next fix:** add restrained wear between the steps and practice ground, around the dummy, and immediately in front of the rack. Ground lanterns, bamboo, basin, and wall feet with small, asset-specific contact treatments. Do not turn every connection into a paved path.

### 5. The stepping stones look stamped

The identical pale slab repeats with regular spacing. Several slabs disappear beneath the foreground roof, so the initial view shows disconnected fragments.

**Next fix:** introduce two or three closely related slab variants with slight changes in chipped corners, wear, and surface value. Check the visible sequence with the foreground roof present. Keep their low, flat profile and coherent axis.

## Architecture

### 6. The enclosure visually overpowers the hall — first priority

The continuous bright plaster, red framing, and dark tiled roofs create a strong picture frame. The small hall must compete with architecture surrounding almost the entire image.

**Next fix:** reduce contrast and repetitive detail in the boundary walls, especially the foreground band and side roofs. Keep the hall's dimensions and identity; establish hierarchy through surrounding values and detail density.

**Acceptance:** the hall is the main architectural focal point at a glance, with the perimeter reading as supporting enclosure.

### 7. Side-wall roofs and frontal roof caps imply different depths

The side roofs form long, deep diagonal bands with thin visible wall faces. The north and south roofs have shallower frontal profiles. At their junctions, the change in projection remains conspicuous.

**Next fix:** review the enclosure as one overhead-perspective structure. Establish consistent roof overhang, ridge/eave relationships, post thickness, and wall-foot height before adjusting individual corner pixels. Check all four corners together.

**Acceptance:** the returns read as the same wall turning a corner, without sudden changes in apparent material scale or roof mass.

### 8. The southern opening is visually swallowed by the continuous roof

The foreground cap runs across almost the full width of the courtyard. The opening is mainly established by the missing wall panel beneath it; its roof treatment barely distinguishes a gateway. The broad cap hides much of a character passing under it.

**Next fix:** resolve the gate as a deliberate structural opening: two clear jambs, a credible beam/roof relationship, and clean wall terminations. Review the roof depth and silhouette in relation to the passage. Avoid solving this by stacking extra roof fragments or posts.

### 9. The hall feels embedded in the rear boundary

Its facade overlaps the same horizontal band as the rear wall, with little visible side depth or ground separation. It can read as an ornate gateway in the enclosure rather than a hall standing against it.

**Next fix:** clarify the existing junctions at the hall's outer eaves, sill, and adjoining wall ends. Use restrained contact shadow and ground treatment to distinguish its footprint. Preserve the hall artwork instead of stretching it or duplicating facade pieces.

### 10. The deck and stair need a clearer shared construction

The broad veranda, shallow central steps, small supports, and strong horizontal board edges compete at a small scale. Their grounding is less convincing than the main facade.

**Next fix:** simplify the readable support rhythm and align the stair, deck front, building sill, and ground contact. Review the outer deck wings as carefully as the center. The intended rise should be understandable from the artwork alone.

### 11. The hall roof has too little framing space

The upper ornaments press against the top of the image, while the foreground enclosure occupies a substantial lower band. This compresses the small hall vertically within a heavy border.

**Next fix:** judge roof headroom and foreground mass together in the full 1280 × 720 composition. Preserve the hall scale; consider layout/framing adjustments only after quieting the enclosure.

## Props

### 12. Props do not yet share a consistent visual finish — first priority

The sword rack uses very thin, bright silhouettes, the dummy uses dense straw detail, the basin is small and shallow, and the lantern has a strongly outlined heavy cap. The difference in detail density makes them feel sourced separately.

**Next fix:** compare all props beside the player at native scale on the same grass background. Align outline strength, pixel-cluster size, highlight contrast, and upper-left lighting. Compare physical parts—sword grips, dummy head, basin rim—not just total image heights.

**Acceptance:** props belong to the same art set without all becoming equally large, dark, or detailed.

### 13. The sword rack needs stronger construction and clearer swords

At game scale, the thin blades and narrow supports are more prominent than the rack's structure. It reads as a row of spikes before it reads as equipment held by a wooden stand.

**Next fix:** improve the relationship between grips, guards, blades, and supports. Make the support rails and feet readable, reduce isolated bright pixels, and check believable sword length beside the current characters. Keep it compact.

### 14. The dummy needs a clearer target silhouette

The current dummy is broader than older versions, but its small head, several narrow straw bundles, and thin stand compete for attention. At a glance it can still read as a decorative straw post.

**Next fix:** simplify the torso into a stronger mass, make the head/shoulder relationship clearer, and separate the target body from the support. Retain the straw material with grouped texture rather than many fine strands.

### 15. The basin is weakly grounded and difficult to identify

It reads as a small dark oval with a pale rim and a thin projecting detail. On uniform grass, it can look like a loose bowl or discarded object rather than a permanent courtyard fixture.

**Next fix:** clarify the rim, water surface, and ladle silhouette. Establish a restrained base/contact shadow or modest worn placement area. Check scale against a character's feet and hands before enlarging it.

### 16. The bamboo repeats a recognizable silhouette

Both clumps share the same large trunk and leaf arrangement, despite their staggered placement. Their size makes the duplication conspicuous. Their base treatment is also sharply separated from the otherwise uniform grass.

**Next fix:** create one related silhouette variant with different culm heights and leaf grouping. Integrate the bases with small, irregular ground contacts. Preserve the restrained two-clump composition.

### 17. Ground contact and lighting need one shared pass

The architecture, lantern, bamboo, rack, dummy, rocks, and basin have different strengths of baked shading and contact definition. Some look firmly seated, while others look placed on top of the terrain image.

**Next fix:** review all bases under the established upper-left light. Use compact contact shadows and consistent material values; avoid adding large new cast shadows that disagree with existing art.

## Next iteration order

1. Correct the dirt silhouette, grass/soil transition, and broad ground texture.
2. Quiet the perimeter and resolve enclosure projection, corners, and gate construction.
3. Clarify the hall/deck/wall joins without changing the original hall scale.
4. Harmonize props, prioritizing rack readability, dummy mass, and basin grounding.
5. Finish subtle wear, stone variants, bamboo variation, and contact shadows.

Review each pass at native 1280 × 720 with characters visible. Use enlarged crops to inspect joins, but approve scale, contrast, and hierarchy from the full game view. Preserve the sparse yard, readable open practice space, and coherent central alignment.
